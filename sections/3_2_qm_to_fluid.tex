\subsection{From the Schrödinger equation to the Euler equations: A path of model reduction}

Using simulations we can study the evolution of systems 
under given physical laws. The first step in designing a simulation
thus is to choose a physical model striking a balance between
accuracy and computational cost, where based on appropriate assumptions
the model order might be reduced.

\subsubsection{Towards kinetic gas theory}
For a small ensemble of $N$ particles
we might be able to model the full $3N$ dimensional wave function
(ignoring inner degrees of freedom) governed by the Schrödinger equation, 
but it will quickly be computationally unfeasible.

In a first model order reduction step we might decouple electrons and protons
(Born-Oppenheimer approximation, \cite{gherghe25}). Density functional
theory exactly reformulates the Schrödinger equation for the
many-electron system in terms of the simpler electron density
but comes with an intractable exchange correlation functional,
where, however, recent progress has been made using machine learning \citep{luise25}. 
Going further we model atoms
with effective interatomic potentials (like the Lennard-Jones potential)
for molecular dynamics (MD) simulations with which up to $20 \cdot 10^{12}$
atoms have been simulated \citep{twetris}. While very impressive, $20 \cdot 10^{12}$
atoms is still tiny compared to a single mole with $\sim 6\cdot10^{23}$ particles which for
an ideal gas would correspond to a simulation volume of $\sim 22.4$ liters. As a next
simplifying assumption we assume the \textit{free flight} between interactions to be much longer
than the interaction time \citep{clausius58} and model \textit{molecule-particles} with an effective interaction
radius which only exhibit collisions - we have arrived at kinetic gas theory.\footnote{
As a remark to the historically interested reader: The paper cited, i.e. \cite{clausius58}, defends 
the \textit{free flight} assumption (\enquote{daß die Theile des von einem Molecüle 
beschriebenen Weges, auf welchem die Molecularkräfte von Einfluß sind, 
indem sie die Bewegung des Molecüls in Richtung oder 
Geschwindigkeit merklich ändern, gegen die Theile des Weges, 
aufwelchem die Krafte als unwirksam betrachtet werden können, verschwinden.}) against Buijs-Ballot's
criticism regarding the pace with which gases mix
(\enquote{Wie kommt es denn, daß Tabacksrauch sich in Zimmern so lange in unbewegten Lagen ausbreitet?}).}
Note that when modeling 
the interatomic or intermolecular potentials, at a sufficiently large collision parameter, particles
perform a kind of slingshot maneuver (attractive potential), which can never be modeled 
in a pure collisional system. But this can be fine - with the right parameters our system can
work globally in spite of local differences. 

\subsubsection{Hilbert's sixth problem: from hard spheres to the Euler equations}

The question on how to rigorously go from the \enquote{atomistic view to the laws 
of motion of continua} \citep{hilbert19}, stated as part of Hilbert's sixth problem,
remains the focus of ongoing research \citep{deng25}. The problem can be split into
two parts, first the kinetic limit from hard sphere dynamics to modeling a one-particle
phase space distribution and second, the hydrodynamic limit towards modeling macroscopic
quantities which will under further assumptions result in the Euler equations.

\subsubsubsection{Kinetic limit}
Consider $N$ hard spheres of
diameter $\epsilon$ in a $d$-dimensional space. In the kinetic limit 
\begin{equation}
    N \rightarrow \infty, \quad N\epsilon^{d-1} = \alpha = \mathcal{O}(1) \quad \text{(Boltzmann grad scaling law)}
    \label{eq:boltzmann_grad}
\end{equation}
with some constant $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}$, one can describe the particle ensemble
in terms of a one-particle phase-space density distribution function $f(\vec{x}, \vec{u},t)$ (position $\vec{x}$, velocity $\vec{u}$, time $t$)
defined so that
\begin{equation}
    f(\vec{x},\vec{u},t) d^3x \, d^3u
\end{equation}
is (depending on the normalization) the probability of finding a particle in the
phase space volume $d^3xd^3u$ around $(\vec{x},\vec{u})$ at time $t$ or the expected
number or particles therein, so in total
\begin{equation}
    N = \int \int f(\vec{x},\vec{u},t) d^3x \, d^3u, \quad \text{total number of particles } N
\end{equation}
whose dynamics is governed by the Boltzmann equation \citep{bodin}, which when also including
an external acceleration $\vec{g}$ is given by
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d}{dt} f(\vec{x},\vec{u},t) = \partial_t f + \vec{\dot{x}} \vec{\nabla}_\vec{x} f + \vec{\dot{u}} \vec{\nabla}_\vec{u} f = \left. \frac{df}{dt} \right|_c, \quad \text{with } \vec{\dot{x}} = \vec{u}, \vec{\dot{u}} = \vec{g}
\end{equation}  
\citep{bodin} where $\left. \frac{df}{dt} \right|_c$ is the change in $f$ due to \textit{collisions}.
$\left. \frac{df}{dt} \right|_c$ can be understood in the sense of discontinuous motion (instantaneous velocity changes)
kicking a particle out of a phase space volume. The details and exact conditions of deriving
the Boltzmann equation from hard sphere dynamics are very intricate and go far beyond this thesis,
here we only refer to \cite{deng24}. Interestingly, while hard sphere dynamics are time-reversible,
the dynamics described by the Boltzmann equation are not (the entropy functional 
increases in time), the arrow of time emerges \citep{ardourel17,deng24,villani2008}.

\greybox{
\textbf{Sidenote on the Lattice Boltzmann Method (LBM):} Given the Boltzmann equation with 
a BGK (Bhatnagar-Gross-Krook) collision operator (without external forces),
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \vec{u} \cdot \nabla f = -\frac{1}{\tau} \left(f - f_{\text{eq}}\right),
\end{equation}
where $f_{\text{eq}}$ is the local equilibrium distribution, we can discretize physical 
space into a lattice of cells and velocity space into a finite set of discrete velocity vectors on the grid. 
By appropriately choosing the details of the numerical scheme, one can recover the isothermal 
Navier–Stokes equations (continuity and momentum, with energy decoupled) from the discrete 
Boltzmann dynamics, up to an error of order $\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{Ma}^2)$, 
where $\mathrm{Ma}$ is the Mach number (the ratio of the bulk fluid motion to the signal speed in the fluid, the sound speed) \citep{kruger17, flatscher19}. This error bounds the method to 
weakly compressible flows, typically with $\mathrm{Ma} \lesssim 0.3$, but extensions for higher 
Mach numbers have been developed \citep{liu19}. The LBM is highly parallelizable \citep{ataei2024xlb} and
we provide a didactic implementation in \texttt{JAX} under \href{https://github.com/leo1200/micro-lbm}{https://github.com/leo1200/micro-lbm}.
}

\subsubsubsection{Hydrodynamic limit}
Rather than describing a $6$ dimensional phase space density we would
like to obtain governing equations for physically meaningful fields, such as the mass density distribution.
In the hydrodynamic limit of the collision rate going to infinity, corresponding to $\alpha \rightarrow \infty$ in Eq. \ref{eq:boltzmann_grad}, practically a mean free path
$\lambda_{\text{mfp}}\ll L$ (system size $L$), such hydrodynamic equations can be derived, namely the
Euler and the Navier-Stokes equations \citep{raymond09,deng25}. The central idea
in formal derivations like \cite{deng25} is that in the limit of $\alpha \rightarrow \infty$ local Maxwellians

\begin{equation}
    \mathfrak{M}=\mathfrak{M}(t, x, v)=\frac{\rho(t, x)}{(2 \pi T(t, x))^{d / 2}} e^{-\frac{|v-u(t, x)|^2}{2 T(t, x)}}
\end{equation}

will approximately solve the Boltzmann equation given that the macroscopic quantities $(\rho,\vec{u},T)$ fulfill
the corresponding hydrodynamical equations.

By taking the moments of the distribution function over velocity space
\begin{equation}
    M_n(\vec{x},t) = \int \vec{v}^n f(\vec{x},\vec{u},t) \, d^3u
\end{equation}
quantities of interest are obtained
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textcolor{blue1}{density} (0th moment)
    \begin{equation}
         n = \int f(\vec{x},\vec{u},t) \, d^3u, \quad \rho = nm, \quad \text{particle mass } m
    \end{equation}
    \item \textcolor{blue1}{mean velocity} (1st moment)
    \item \textcolor{blue1}{pressure tensor} as of the fluctuations of the velocity around the mean (2nd moment)
    \item \textcolor{blue1}{heat tensor} (3rd moment)
\end{itemize}
In a less rigorous manner compared to \cite{deng25}, the development of those quantities given by balance (/conservation) equations can be obtained by
taking the appropriate moments of the Boltzmann equation (mass, momentum and energy are conserved).

These balance equations can be seen as the local formulations of macroscopic balances, which are justified
under the hydrodynamic limit, i.e. we can define sufficiently small fluid volumes on which the fluid
quantities are still well-defined.

\subsection{Emergence of irreversibility in the Boltzmann equation}
Consider a classical simulation of colliding spheres (interaction time $\ll$ free flight time), where
we start out with the particles concentrated at the center of our box. As of their thermal motion,
the particles will spread out and fill the box. Now consider we start with this end state
and reverse all velocities - the particles will clump up - anti-dissipation. Such things can
happen miscroscopically, they are simply very unlikely.
\yellowbox{Something unlikely miscroscopically is virtually impossible macroscopically.}
But based on the Boltzmmann equation, this will never happen - the Boltzmann equation is
\textit{irreversible} and will never show anti-dissipation. 
% So when we later discritize
% the Boltzmann equation and use it to forward our system, we can simulate macroscopically
% realistic behavior without lots of particles.
\yellowbox{\enquote{The derivation of the Boltzmann equation (BE) from the Hamiltonian equations of motion of 
a hard spheres gas is a key topic on irreversibility (Sklar 1993, p.32; Uffink 2007, Section 4). 
Although the Hamiltonian equations of motion are invariant under time reversal, the BE is not. Moreover, 
this equation allows us to derive the H-theorem, which states that a function H monotonically decreases with
 time, and thus, that the minus-H function increases, in agreement with the second law of thermodynamics. The 
 derivation of the BE thus raises the question of irreversibility, since this equation exhibits irreversibility 
 even though the microscopic description of the gas is based on reversible equations.} from \cite{ardourel17}
 where the emergence of irreversibility is discussed in detail.}

We can try to understand the emergence of irreversibility based on going from
the exact information of the positions and momenta of the particles to an averaged
phase space density:

\greenbox{\enquote{In other words, when the particles are described by the Boltzmann equation, our knowledge is incomplete, since the positions and momenta of all the particles remain unknown, in contrast to the description by means of Hamilton canonical equations or Liouville equation. And this lack of knowledge makes the evolution of the one-particle distribution function f irreversible. The irreversibility is then explicitly expressed by the collision integral in the Boltzmann equation. The second law of thermodynamics thus emerges from completely reversible dynamics when our description is incomplete (not seeing all positions and momenta of the particles).} from \cite{Kincl23}}
