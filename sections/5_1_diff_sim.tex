\section{A Primer on Differentiable Simulations}
\label{sec:diff_sim}
\thispagestyle{plain}

We are interested in differentiating simulation outputs $\vec{U}(\vec{\theta})$ or an objective function
$J(\vec{\theta})$ of these simulation outputs with respect to the simulation parameters $\vec{\theta}$, e.g. for sensitivity
analysis or optimization tasks. These parameters might include the weights and biases of
neural networks situated inside the simulator for error correction \citep{um21} or capturing
physical effects (e.g. \cite{wang23}). Generally speaking, our simulator is the implementation
of a numerical method for solving an ordinary differential equation (ODE) 
or partial differential equation (PDE).

There are essentially four ways for computing these derivatives:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{finite differencing:} We can approximate derivatives based on finite differences,
    e.g. the forward difference
    \begin{equation}
        \partial_{\theta_i} U_j = \frac{U_j(\vec{\theta} + h \vec{e}_i) - U_j(\vec{\theta})}{h} + \mathcal{O}(h)
    \end{equation}
    or central difference
    \begin{equation}
        \partial_{\theta_i} U_j = \frac{U_j(\vec{\theta} + h \vec{e}_i) - U_j(\vec{\theta} - h \vec{e}_i)}{2h} + \mathcal{O}(h^2)
    \end{equation}
    with some $h>0$. To calculate these numerical derivatives we only
    need to evaluate our simulator for different parameter choices.
    Finite differencing, however, has multiple problems. While we would like to make $h$ small to reduce
    the truncation error, e.g. $\mathcal{O}(h^2)$ in the central difference scheme, small $h$ will come at the cost
    of round-off errors due to limited floating point precision - so we have to strike a balance (see e.g. Fig. 3 in
    \cite{baydin18}). While we only have to evaluate our simulator twice for forward or central differences
    of all simulation outputs with respect to one simulation parameter, computing each additional 
    derivative with respect to a parameter requires one 
    (forward differencing) or two (central differencing) additional simulations.
    This will be unfeasible in many-parameter settings such as fitting a neural network inside the simulator.
    \item \textbf{automatic differentiation (AD):} If the building blocks of our simulator
    are differentiable, we can differentiate through our simulator to machine precision by applying the
    chain rule, more specifically forward- or reverse-mode automatic differentiation \citep{baydin18}.
    These building blocks might be the primitive operations in a numerical library like \texttt{JAX}
    \citep{jax} or more monolithic numerical operators with hand-calculated derivatives \citep[e.g. of an advection step in][Sec. 13]{thuerey25}. While calculating the derivatives of a simulation
    outcome with respect to multiple parameters is accurate, fast and simple using
    automatic differentiation, memory demand can be an issue \citep[Sec. 5.1.1.2]{diffrax}.
    We will discuss automatic differentiation in greater detail below.
    \item \textbf{solving an adjoint problem:} The derivatives of interest can also be obtained
    by solving an adjoint equation of the PDE or ODE of the physical state. The state and adjoint
    system will generally differ, e.g. to calculate the derivative of the solution to an initial
    value problem to a PDE with a first order derivative in time, solving the adjoint problem
    amounts to integrating a final value problem backwards in time \citep{wilcox13}. The adjoint equation
    might be discretized independently of the state equation or derived based on the discretized state
    equation. While \textit{adjoint methods} do not have the memory problems
    of automatic differentiation, deriving the adjoint system might be tedious, and
    numerically solving the adjoint system comes at additional
    computational cost and also introduces numerical errors to the derivatives calculated (see
    Sec. 5.1.2.3 and Example 5.6 in \cite{diffrax}). The problem of inaccurate
    derivatives can be amplified if different discretizations of the state and adjoint system
    are chosen \citep{wilcox13}. In the following we will limit ourselves to the continuous
    adjoint method for ODEs, where an adjoint system is derived from the ODE system itself, not a discretization
    thereof. We limit ourselves to ODEs for two reasons: First, to make the introduction more approachable and 
    second because PDEs with dependencies on space and time can be turned into a system of ODEs in time by discretizing
    over space \citep[\textit{method of lines},][Sec. 2.1]{bradley24}. For specific coverage on adjoints for
    linear hyperbolic PDEs (solved with a discontinuous Galerkin method), we refer to
    Sec. 2 in \cite{wilcox13}.
    \item \textbf{Reversible solvers}: If the numerical solver is reversible, we can faithfully reconstruct the
    information necessary for the backward pass during the backward pass, but current reversible solvers
    are low-order and have poor stability properties \citep[Sec. 5.1.3]{diffrax}.
\end{itemize}

Our fluid simulator, \texttt{jf1uids}, is differentiable based on automatic differentiation
in \texttt{JAX}. We will therefore introduce automatic differentiation in the following.
For reference, we will also present the continuous adjoint method and compare both
at the example of optimizing parameters in an ODE.

\subsection{Introducing Automatic Differentiation}

Consider we are interested in the derivatives of a function $f: \mathbb{R}^n \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^m$,
e.g. the simulation output as a function of the parameters.

Based on the chain rule and known derivatives of a set of primitive operations,
we can efficiently and accurately (to machine precision) calculate these derivatives.

\greenbox{
    Intuitively
    \begin{itemize}
        \item forward-mode automatic differentiation forward propagates
        a perturbation $\vec{v} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ at $\vec{x}$ (with $\vec{v}$ sufficiently small
        so we can linearize $\vec{f}$ around $\vec{x}$) in the input space through the computational graph of the function $\vec{f}$ to
        a resulting perturbation $\left.\mat{J}_\vec{f}\right|_{\vec{x}} \vec{v}$ in the output space
        \item backward-mode automatic differentiation relates output perturbations back to 
        input perturbations, projecting perturbations in the output space
        $\vec{p} \in \mathbb{R}^m$ onto the output perturbations caused by the base vectors of
        the input space

        \begin{equation}
            \left.\mat{J}_\vec{f}^T\right|_{\vec{x}} \vec{p} =
            \begin{pmatrix}
            - & (\partial_{x_1} \vec{f})^T & - \\
            - & (\partial_{x_2} \vec{f})^T & - \\
            & \vdots & \\
            - & (\partial_{x_n} \vec{f})^T & -
            \end{pmatrix}
            \vec{p}
            = \begin{pmatrix}
            (\partial_{x_1} \vec{f})^T \vec{p} \\
            (\partial_{x_2} \vec{f})^T \vec{p} \\
            \vdots \\
            (\partial_{x_n} \vec{f})^T \vec{p}
            \end{pmatrix}
        \end{equation}

        for a scalar output and $p = 1$ this amounts to the derivatives of this
        output with respect to all inputs.
    \end{itemize}
}

\subsubsection{Forward-mode automatic differentiation}

In a simulation we typically start at initial conditions $\vec{x} \in \mathbb{R}^N$ on which
we apply a chain of transformations, e.g.

\begin{equation}
    \vec{f} = \vec{g}_m \circ \cdots \circ \vec{g}_2 \circ \vec{g}_1, \quad \vec{f}(\vec{x}) = \vec{g}_m(\dots \vec{g}_2(\vec{g}_1(\vec{x})))
\end{equation}

with for simplicity $\vec{f},\vec{g}_m,\cdots,\vec{g}_1: \mathbb{R}^N \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^N$. We might be interested in
the sensitivity of the simulation output to a wiggle $\vec{v}$ in the initial conditions as captured
by the Jacobian vector product (JVP) $\mat{J}_\vec{f}(\vec{x}) \vec{v}$ with $\mat{J}_\vec{f}(\vec{x}) \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$
if we linearize $\vec{f}$ around $\vec{x}$.

By the chain rule

\begin{equation}
    \mat{J}_\vec{f}(\vec{x}) = \mat{J}_{\vec{g}_m}(\vec{g}_{m-1},\cdots, g_1(\vec{x})) \cdots \mat{J}_{\vec{g}_2}(\vec{g}_1(\vec{x})) \mat{J}_{\vec{g}_1}(\vec{x})
\end{equation}

which we can calculate in $m - 1$ matrix-matrix multiplications, each scaling with $\mathcal{O}(N^3)$ (naively) (or e.g. $\mathcal{O}(N^{\log_2 7})$ with Strassen's algorithm \citep{strassen69}),
leading to an overall scaling of calculating the Jacobian of $\mathcal{O}(mN^3)$. But for a JVP
we don't actually need to compute the Jacobian, we can just evaluate

\begin{equation}
    \mat{J}_\vec{f}(\vec{x}) \vec{v} = \mat{J}_{\vec{g}_m}(\vec{g}_{m-1}(\cdots(g_1(\vec{x})))) \cdots \mat{J}_{\vec{g}_2}(\vec{g}_1(\vec{x})) \mat{J}_{\vec{g}_1}(\vec{x}) \vec{v}
\end{equation}

from right to left with $m-1$ matrix-vector products, each costing only $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$!

If we capture the dependency structure of a composition of functions as a computational graph,
with functions as nodes and dependencies as arrows, calculating the JVP amounts to
\begin{itemize}
    \item starting from the inputs evaluate the nodes to calculate the inputs to the next nodes (\textit{primals}) in a forward pass
    \item in the same forward pass, starting at a \textit{tangent} $\vec{v}$ in the input space calculate the JVPs of the nodes (evaluated at the \textit{primals}), giving the \textit{tangents} to the following nodes
\end{itemize}

Forward differentiating through computational graphs is commonly called forward-mode
automatic differentiation\footnote{We will also use \textit{autodiff} to refer to automatic differentiation.}, forwardpropagation, or, drawing from differential geometry
terminology, pushforward.\footnote{More details regarding the relation to
differential geometry can be found under \href{https://juliadiff.org/ChainRulesCore.jl/stable/maths/propagators.html}{https://juliadiff.org/ChainRulesCore.jl/stable/maths/propagators.html}.}

\greenbox{Partial derivatives $\partial_{x_i}\vec{f}$ are JVPs with the basis vectors, $\partial_{x_i}\vec{f} = \mat{J}_\vec{f}\vec{\hat{e}}_i$.}

\subsubsubsection{Example of forward differentiation through a computational graph}

Consider the following composition of functions

\begin{equation}
    \vec{f}(\vec{x}) = 
    \begin{pmatrix}
        g(h(x_1,x_2)) \\
        u(h(x_1, x_2), x_2)
    \end{pmatrix}
    \label{eq:example_composition}
\end{equation}

with some functions $g: \mathbb{R} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}, h: \mathbb{R}^2 \rightarrow \mathbb{R}, u: \mathbb{R}^2 \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$.
As $\vec{f}$ is a composition of functions, a chain of dependencies,
it makes sense to illustrate it in a computational graph with functions as nodes and dependencies
as arrows, as done in Fig. \ref{fig:comp_graph_diff}.

\begin{figure}[!htb]

 \caption{Illustrated is the computational graph of a function $\vec{f}: \mathbb{R}^2 \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^2$ (given in Eq. \ref{eq:example_composition})
 which is composed of functions $g: \mathbb{R} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}, h: \mathbb{R}^2 \rightarrow \mathbb{R}, u: \mathbb{R}^2 \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$
 depicted as nodes in the computational graph. Input relations are depicted as arrows.}
 \label{fig:comp_graph_diff}
\end{figure}

Consider a Jacobian-vector-product (JVP), here for our example function $\vec{f}$

\begin{equation}
    \mat{J}_\vec{f} \vec{v} = \begin{pmatrix}
                                (\partial_1 h)\,(\partial_1 g) & (\partial_2 h)\,(\partial_1 g) \\
                                (\partial_1 h)\,(\partial_1 u) & (\partial_2 u) + (\partial_2 h)\,(\partial_1 u)
                              \end{pmatrix} \vec{v}
                            = \begin{pmatrix}
                                (v_1\,\partial_1 h + v_2\,\partial_2 h)\,\partial_1 g \\
                                (v_1\,\partial_1 h + v_2\,\partial_2 h)\,\partial_1 u + v_2\,\partial_2 u
                              \end{pmatrix}
    \label{eq:example_jvp}
\end{equation}

where $\partial_i$ denotes the partial derivative of a function with respect to its $i$-th argument.

Calculating the JVP through the computational graph is illustrated in Fig. \ref{fig:forward_diff}.

\subsubsection{Reverse-mode automatic differentiation}
Let us reconsider our simulation $\vec{f} = \vec{g}_m \circ \cdots \circ \vec{g}_2 \circ \vec{g}_1$ from before
but we apply a scalar function $L: \mathbb{R}^N \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ to the simulation output $\vec{f}(\vec{x})$,
e.g. a loss $L(\vec{f}(\vec{x})) = ||\vec{f}(\vec{x}) - \vec{y}_{exp}||^2$ to an experimental measurement $\vec{y}_{exp} \in \mathbb{R}^N$.

To optimize $L$, we are interested in the gradient $\vec{\nabla}_\vec{x}L$, which we could calculate in $N$ forward
differentiations (for each base vector $\vec{\hat{e}}_i, i = 1,...,N$).

Let us write out this gradient

\begin{equation}
    \vec{\nabla}_\vec{x}L(\vec{f}(\vec{x})) = \mat{J}_\vec{f}^T \vec{\nabla}_\vec{f} L =  \mat{J}_{\vec{g}_1}^T(\vec{x}) \mat{J}_{\vec{g}_2}^T(\vec{g}_1(\vec{x})) \cdots \mat{J}_{\vec{g}_m}^T(\vec{g}_{m-1}(\cdots(g_1(\vec{x})))) \vec{\nabla}_\vec{f} L
\end{equation}

We see that calculating the gradient amounts to a Jacobian transpose vector product (J'VP). Similar
to the forward-mode automatic differentiation rather than calculating the Jacobian matrix explicitly by matrix
multiplication, we perform a chain of matrix vector products, here J'VPs. Note that the 
transpose has permuted the order of the Jacobians - we now move backwards through our computational graph.

\yellowbox{To evaluate the Jacobians, we need the evaluations $\vec{g}_1,...\vec{g}_{m-1}$ obtained in a \textit{forward pass}
through the computational graph. Therefore, \textbf{reverse-mode autodiff has memory complexity
scaling linearly with the number of algorithm iterations}, which can become a bottleneck.}

\greenbox{We can calculate the gradient of a scalar function in a single backward pass through the computational graph
(which must be preceded by a forward pass).}

If we capture the dependency structure of a composition of functions as a computational graph,
with functions as nodes and dependencies as arrows, calculating the J'VP amounts to
\begin{itemize}
    \item in a forward pass, evaluate all nodes, possibly precalculate the Jacobians of the nodes with respect to their inputs
    \item starting from the output space calculate the J'VP backwards through the computational graph
\end{itemize}

Backward differentiation through a computational graph like this is commonly called reverse-mode
automatic differentiation, backpropagation, or, again drawing from differential geometry terminology,
pullback (of a differential form).

\subsubsubsection{Example of backward differentiation through a computational graph}

We consider the same example as before (Eq. \ref{eq:example_composition}). The J'VP reads

\begin{equation}
    \mat{J}_\vec{f}^T \vec{s} = \begin{pmatrix}
                                (\partial_1 h)\,(\partial_1 g) & (\partial_2 h)\,(\partial_1 g) \\
                                (\partial_1 h)\,(\partial_1 u) & (\partial_2 u) + (\partial_2 h)\,(\partial_1 u)
                              \end{pmatrix}^T \vec{s}
                            = \begin{pmatrix}
                                (\partial_1 h)\,((\partial_1 g) s_1 + (\partial_1 u) s_2) \\
                                (\partial_2 h)\,((\partial_1 g) s_1 + (\partial_1 u) s_2) + (\partial_2 u) s_2
                              \end{pmatrix} 
    \label{eq:example_vjp}
\end{equation}

which is illustrated on the computational graph in Fig. \ref{fig:backward_diff}. Often, one considers
the transpose of the J'VP $(\mat{J}_\vec{f}^T \vec{s})^T=\vec{s}^T \mat{J}_\vec{f}$ and calls
it Vector-Jacobian Product (VJP).

\subsubsubsection{Checkpointing for memory efficiency}
\label{sec:checkpointing}
Consider we have created a computational graph of our simulation 
with primitive operations as nodes in the graph. To calculate the J'VPs 
in the backward pass, the \textit{linearization points}, e.g. all inputs to
(elementwise nonlinear) primitive operations, must be known - this saves
space compared to the naive approach of storing the Jacobians of the primitive
operations.

It can, however, be too costly memorywise to store or too costly computationally (with regards
to the computational cost of memory access)
to retrieve all these linearization points.

Alternatively, we might only store certain \textit{checkpoints} and recompute intermediate 
linearization points, e.g. for a larger function we might only store its inputs and
calculate the linearization points of its primitive operations by reevaluating the
function in the backward pass as necessary.

\greybox{\textbf{A note on optimal checkpointing:} Let us consider a simulation of $l=6$ timesteps
with states $x_0\rightarrow x_1 \rightarrow \cdots \rightarrow x_6$ and assume we are able to store
$c = 2$ checkpoints at a time. We might reasonably start with $x_0$ and $x_3$ checkpointed, but in
the backward pass might want to change which state is checkpointed for the optimal checkpointing
strategy. This is illustrated in Fig. \ref{fig:checkpointed_backprop}, an adaptation of Fig. 2.1 in \cite{stumm10}. But how do we
choose initial checkpoints when the number of simulation steps is unknown a priori, i.e. in adaptive
time-stepping with a while-loop? For this there exist optimal \textit{online} checkpointing strategies,
e.g. Algorithm 1 from \cite{stumm10}.\footnote{An implementation in \texttt{JAX} by Patrick
Kidger is available under \url{https://github.com/patrick-kidger/equinox/blob/main/equinox/internal/\_loop/checkpointed.py}}
The algorithmic details are beyond the scope of this thesis, but in Fig. \ref{fig:online_checkpointing} adapted from
Fig. 2.2 in \cite{stumm10} we provide intuition on how one adapts which states are checkpointed
while moving forward in the loop.
}

More information on checkpointing in the context of \texttt{JAX} can be found under
\url{https://docs.jax.dev/en/latest/\_autosummary/jax.checkpoint.html}.

\subsubsection{Example of custom differentiation rules: Implicit differentiation through a
fixed-point iteration}
\label{sec:custom_diff}

There might be parts of our simulator for which we know differentiation rules so that
we can avoid the memory and computational cost of differentiating through the primitive
operations making up these parts.

To equip a function with custom rules for forward and backward differentiation, we
need to define a custom JVP and J'VP, here its transpose the VJP, respectively.

As an example, consider our simulation contains a fixed point iteration, approximating
$\vec{x}^\star(\vec{\theta})$ implicitly defined by

\begin{equation}
    \vec{x}^\star(\vec{\theta}) = \vec{f}(\vec{x}^\star(\vec{\theta}), \vec{\theta})
\end{equation}

by iterating $\vec{x}_{t+1} = \vec{f}(\vec{x}_t, \vec{\theta})$ (assuming there exists one
attracting fixed-point, which we usually show using Banach's fixed-point theorem).

By the chain rule (assuming $\vec{x}^\star(\vec{\theta}) = \vec{f}(\vec{x}^\star(\vec{\theta}), \vec{\theta})$ holds in a neighborhood around
$\vec{\theta}_{eval}$ around which we want to differentiate)\footnote{The derivation follows \url{https://docs.jax.dev/en/latest/advanced-autodiff.html\#example-implicit-function-differentiation-of-iterative-implementations}.}

\begin{equation}
    \mat{J} = \partial_\vec{\theta} \vec{x}^\star(\vec{\theta}) = \underbrace{\partial_\vec{\theta} \vec{x}^\star(\vec{\theta})}_{=\mat{J}} \underbrace{\partial_1 \vec{f}(\vec{x}^\star(\vec{\theta}), \vec{\theta})}_{=:\mat{A}} + \underbrace{\partial_2 \vec{f}(\vec{x}^\star(\vec{\theta}), \vec{\theta})}_{=:\mat{B}}
    \label{eq:implicit_diff}
\end{equation}

(where we have implicitly applied the implicit function theorem, see \cite{blondel22} for more details on this).

We can rewrite Eq. \ref{eq:implicit_diff} to give an explicit expression

\begin{equation}
    \mat{J} = (\mat{1} - \mat{A})^{-1} \mat{B}
\end{equation}

The JVP can therefore be calculated by solving the linear system

\begin{equation}
    (\mat{1} - \mat{A}) (\mat{J} \vec{v}) = \mat{B} \vec{v}
    \label{eq:jvp_fixed}
\end{equation}

and the VJP by

\begin{equation}
     (\mat{1} - \mat{A})^T \vec{u} = \vec{v} \quad \rightarrow \quad \vec{v}^T \mat{J} = \underbrace{\vec{v}^T (\mat{1} - \mat{A})^{-1}}_{=\vec{u}^T} \mat{B}
    \label{eq:vjp_fixed}
\end{equation}

where solving the linear systems might be done using GMRES \citep{GMRES}. While there 
are sophisticated techniques for inverting a matrix, the inverse can potentially be very dense even if
the matrix itself is not, which would interfere with smart memory optimizations \citep{rackauckas22}.

For the VJP, we might also solve

\begin{equation}
    (\mat{1} - \mat{A})^T \vec{u} = \vec{v} \quad \iff \quad \vec{u}^T = \vec{v}^T + \vec{u}^T \mat{A}
    \label{eq:vjp_fixed_point}
\end{equation}

using a fixed-point iteration.

As a simple example, based on Heron's root finding algorithm, we construct

\begin{equation}
    \vec{f}(\vec{x}, \vec{\theta}) = \alpha \vec{x} + (1-\alpha) \begin{pmatrix}
        \frac{1}{x_1} \exp{(\theta_1^2 + 2\theta_2)} \\
        \frac{1}{x_2} \exp{(2\theta_1 + \theta_2^2)}
    \end{pmatrix}
    \label{eq:example_fixed_point_equation}
\end{equation}

with analytical solution

\begin{equation}
    \vec{x}^\star(\vec{\theta}) = \begin{pmatrix}
        \exp{(\frac{1}{2}\theta_1^2 + \theta_2)} \\
        \exp{(\theta_1 + \frac{1}{2}\theta_2^2)}
    \end{pmatrix}
    \label{eq:example_fixed_point}
\end{equation}

where we use $\alpha = 0.8$ for slower convergence.\footnote{
The fixed point method is based on
\begin{equation}
    x = \alpha x + (1-\alpha)\frac{S}{x} \iff x = \sqrt{S}
\end{equation}
with $S>0$, known as Heron's method for $\alpha = 0.5$.
It converges with
\begin{equation}
    e_{n+1} = x_{n+1} - \sqrt{S} \underbrace{=}_{x_n = \sqrt{S} + e_n} \alpha (\sqrt{S} + e_n) + (1-\alpha)\frac{\sqrt{S}}{1 + \frac{e_n}{\sqrt{S}}} - \sqrt{S} 
\end{equation}
where approximating $\frac{1}{1 + \frac{e_n}{\sqrt{S}}}$ to 2nd order around $e_n = 0$ to $1 - \frac{e_n}{\sqrt{S}} + \frac{e_n^2}{S}$ yields
\begin{equation}
    e_{n+1} \approx (2\alpha-1) e_n + (1-\alpha)\frac{e_n^2}{\sqrt{S}}
\end{equation}
so only for $\alpha = 0.5$ do we get quadratic convergence, for $\alpha \ne 0.5$ the linear
term dominates and the error decreases by a factor $2\alpha - 1$ per iteration.}

For $v = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\vec{\theta}_{eval} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$
we compare the accuracy of the JVP and VJP compared to the ground thruth between a direct differentiation
through the fixed-point iteration and the implicit differentiation for different numbers of iterations in
Fig. \ref{fig:custom_diffs} with the source code for the custom JVP given in Code-Snippet \ref{code:jvp},
and the custom VJP in Code-Snippet \ref{code:vjp}. The error of the JVP and VJP from direct
automatic differentiation through the fixed-point iteration decreases with the same scaling
as the fixed-point error itself, in the example around two orders of magnitude per $10$ iterations.
This is expected, the automatic differentiation calculates the accurate derivatives of our approximation
of the fixed point to the parameters. Interestingly, the implicit differentiation, derived for
the \textit{true} fixed-points, has better error scaling behavior (roughly $4$ orders of
magnitude per $10$ iterations). By design the reverse-mode automatic differentiation
has a memory overhead compared to the implicit differentiation (scaling with the number of iterations)
while the linear solve (or fixed-point iteration if one follows Eq. \ref{eq:vjp_fixed_point}) 
in the implicit differentiation is a computational overhead compared to the backmode automatic
differentiation (not scaling with the iterations but the number of parameters). We leave
more in-depth profiling to future work.

            def body(_, x): return f(x, theta)
            return fori_loop(0, num_iters, body, x_guess)

        @fixed_point_jvp.defjvp
        def fixed_point_jvp_rule(f, num_iters, primals, tangents):
            x_guess, theta = primals
            dx_guess, dtheta = tangents
            x_star = fixed_point_jvp(f, x_guess, theta, num_iters)
            A = jacobian(f, argnums=0)(x_star, theta)
            B = jacobian(f, argnums=1)(x_star, theta)
            dx = jnp.linalg.solve(jnp.eye(x_star.shape[0]) - A, B @ dtheta)
            # primal output, tangent output
            return x_star, dx
    \end{minted}
    \caption{Implementation of a custom Jacobian vector product for the results of
    fixed-point iterations in \texttt{JAX} following Eq. \ref{eq:jvp_fixed}}
    \label{code:jvp}
\end{codebox}

\subsection{Introducing the Continuous Adjoint Method for ODEs}

We will now introduce the continuous adjoint method for differentiating
the solution of ODEs, or rather a scalar function $L$ of the solution like a loss,
and compare it to direct differentiation through the 
numerical solver in Sec. \ref{sec:ad_vs_adjoint}. Our treatment here follows \cite{diffrax}.

Consider the initial value problem (IVP)

\begin{equation}
    \vec{y}(0) = \vec{y}_0, \quad \frac{d\vec{y}}{dt} = \vec{f}_\vec{\theta}(t,\vec{y}(t))
\end{equation}

with

\begin{equation}
    \vec{y}(0) \in \mathbb{R}^d, \quad \vec{\theta} \in \mathbb{R}^m, \quad \vec{f}_\vec{\theta}: [0,T]\times \mathbb{R}^d \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^d
\end{equation}

where $\vec{y}: [0,T] \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^d$ is the solution to the IVP and $\vec{f}_\vec{\theta}$ is 
continuous in $t$ and uniformly Lipschitz and continuously differentiable in $\vec{y}$.

Our aim is to calculate $\frac{dL(\vec{y}(T))}{d\vec{\theta}}$, where 
$L: \mathbb{R}^d \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ and $T$ is the time point of interest.

This can be achieved by solving the adjoint system (here of the \textit{continuous}
original problem, not its discretization)

\begin{equation}
    \begin{gathered}
        \vec{a}_\vec{y}(T) = \frac{dL}{d\vec{y}(T)}, \quad \frac{d\vec{a}_\vec{y}}{dt}(t) = -\vec{a}_\vec{y}(t)^T \underbrace{\frac{\partial \vec{f}_\vec{\theta}}{\partial \vec{y}}(t,\vec{y}(t))}_{\in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}} \\
        \vec{a}_\vec{\theta}(T) = 0, \quad \frac{d\vec{a}_\vec{\theta}}{dt}(t) = -\vec{a}_\vec{y}(t)^T \underbrace{\frac{\partial \vec{f}_\vec{\theta}}{\partial \vec{\theta}}(t,\vec{y}(t))}_{\in \mathbb{R}^{d \times m}}
    \end{gathered}
    \label{eq:continuous_adjoint}
\end{equation}

with $\vec{a}_\vec{y}: [0,T] \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^d, \vec{a}_\vec{\theta}: [0,T] \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^m$ from $t=T$ to $t = 0$ backwards in time where

\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
        \frac{dL(\vec{y}(T))}{d\vec{\theta}} = \vec{a}_\vec{\theta}(0)
    }
\end{equation}

\yellowbox{Note that to evaluate the Jacobians in Eq. \ref{eq:continuous_adjoint} we need 
to know $\vec{y}(t)$ but to have a memory advantage over direct autodiff through the numerical
solver, we cannot save the whole forward trajectory in the \textit{forward pass}. Therefore,
as we integrate $\vec{a}_\vec{y}$ and $\vec{a}_\vec{\theta}$ backwards in time starting from
$t = T$, we obtain $\vec{y}(t)$ by integrating back from $y(T)$ (obtained in the forward pass).
\textbf{Note, however, that the numerical integration backwards in time will not
match the forward trajectory in general, except when the numerical solver itself is
reversed and floating point errors are insignificant.} Also, the backward problem
might generally be harder than the forward problem.}

For a proof of the continuous adjoint method, we refer to \cite[Sec. 5.1.2.1]{diffrax}.

\subsection{Application Example: AD through the solver
vs. continuous adjoint method on exponential decay}
\label{sec:ad_vs_adjoint}

Consider the ODE for exponential decay

\begin{equation}
    \frac{dy}{dt}(t) = \lambda y(t), \quad \lambda < 0, \quad y(0) = y_0, 
\end{equation}

with analytical solution $y(t) = y_0 \exp{(\lambda t)}$ and analytical
derivative $\partial_\lambda y = t y_0 \exp{(\lambda t)}$.

The adjoint system reads

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        & a_y(T) = 1, \quad && \frac{da_y}{dt}(t) = -a_y(t) \lambda \\
        & a_\lambda(T) = 0, \quad && \frac{da_\lambda}{dt}(t) = -a_y(t) y(t)
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

We will compare the following approaches for calculating $\partial_\lambda y$:

\begin{itemize}
    \item direct automatic differentiation through the implicit Euler method with
    $y_{n+1} = y_n + \Delta t \lambda y_{n+1} \rightarrow y_{n+1} = \frac{y_n}{1-\Delta t \lambda}$
    \item direct automatic differentiation through dopri5\footnote{All tests involving dopri5 were carried out using \texttt{diffrax}, \url{https://docs.kidger.site/diffrax/}.}
    \item the continuous adjoint method with implicit Euler for the forward- and backsolve
    \item a discrete adjoint system of the implicit Euler method which we derive based on 
    the backpropagation idea as illustrated in Fig. \ref{fig:backprop_decay}:
    \begin{equation}
        \begin{gathered}
            g(y,\lambda) = \frac{y}{1-\Delta t \lambda} \rightarrow y_{N} = \underbrace{\left(g \circ g \circ \cdots \circ g\right)}_{\times N}(y_0) \\
            \rightarrow \partial_\lambda y_N = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} \left.\partial_\lambda g \right|_{y_n} a_{y_{n+1}} \text{ with } a_{y_{n}} = \left.\partial_y g \right|_{y_n} a_{y_{n+1}}, a_{y_N} = 1 \\
            \partial_\lambda g = \Delta t \frac{y}{(1 - \Delta t \lambda)^2}, \quad \partial_y g = \frac{1}{1 - \Delta t \lambda}
        \end{gathered}
    \end{equation}
    Let us introduce
    \begin{equation}
        a_{\lambda_n} = a_{\lambda_{n+1}} + \left.\partial_\lambda g \right|_{y_n} a_{y_{n+1}}, \quad a_{\lambda_N} = 0
    \end{equation}
    such that $\partial_\lambda y_N = a_{\lambda_0}$. By manually reverting the implicit Euler step for this specific problem, we find the discrete
    adjoint system
    \begin{equation}
        \begin{gathered}
            y_n = y_{n+1} \cdot (1 - \Delta t \lambda), \quad y_N \text{ from forward pass} \\
            a_{y_{n}} = \frac{1}{1 - \Delta t \lambda} a_{y_{n+1}}, a_{y_N} = 1 \\
            a_{\lambda_n} = a_{\lambda_{n+1}} + \Delta t \frac{y_n}{(1 - \Delta t \lambda)^2} a_{y_{n+1}} = a_{\lambda_{n+1}} + \Delta t a_{y_{n}} y_{n+1}, a_{\lambda_N} = 0
        \end{gathered}
        \label{eq:backprop_decay}
    \end{equation}
    which we can recognize to be a specific discretization of the continuous adjoint equations, connecting
    the continuous adjoint method, backpropagation and reversible solvers.
    \item the continuous adjoint method with dopri5 for the forward- and backsolve
\end{itemize}

    \item the discrete adjoint and autodiff results match as expected
